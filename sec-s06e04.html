    Section 6.4 Episode 4: Proportions

    Episode 4 Learning Goals.

    Set up and solve proportions
    🔗
    🔗

    Episode 4 Key Terminology.

    Proportion
    🔗
    🔗
    Suppose you have a cookie recipe. The recipe requires 2 cups of flour, and makes 24 cookies. How many cups of flour would you need if you wanted to make 36 cookies?
    🔗
    Because the amount of flour we need is unknown, represent it by a variable, \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Old New
    Flour 2 \(x\)
    Cookies 24 36
    This recipe has a certain ratio (see Season 3) of flour to cookies.
    🔗
    From the recipe’s directions, the ratio is:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2\,\,\text{cups of flour}}{24\,\,\text{cookies}} \end{equation*}
    But we want to make 36 cookies, so the ratio that matters to us is:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{x\,\,\text{cups of flour}}{36\,\,\text{cookies}} \end{equation*}
    But since it’s the same recipe, it’s the same ratio:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2\,\,\text{cups of flour}}{24\,\,\text{cookies}} = \frac{x\,\,\text{cups of flour}}{36\,\,\text{cookies}} \end{equation*}
    Now we have an equation that we can solve for \(x\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2}{24}=\frac{x}{36} \end{equation*}
    Multiply both sides by 36 to get rid of the divided 36:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2}{24}\times 36 = \frac{x}{36}\times 36 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{2}{24}\times \frac{36}{1} = x \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{72}{24}=x \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} 3=x \end{equation*}
    In other words, to make 36 cookies with this recipe, we would need 3 cups of flour.
    🔗

    Definition 6.4.1.

    A proportion is an equation that you get by setting two different-looking ratios equal to each other.
    🔗
    🔗
    Proportions arise in situations where you’re working with two related quantities, and doubling Quantity A means doubling Quantity B.
    • When you’re cooking, if you want to double the amount of food you make, you need to double the amount of each ingredient.
      🔗
      🔗
    • When you’re walking at a certain speed, if you want to double the distance you walk, you need to double the time you spend walking.
      🔗
      🔗
    • When you’re being paid at a certain hourly rate, if you want to double the money you earn, you need to double the hours you work.
      🔗
      🔗
    • When you’re buying a certain product, if you want to buy double the number of items, you need to double the amount of money you spend.
      🔗
      🔗
    • When you’re making a photo bigger, if you want to make the double the width of a photo, you also need to double the height of the photo.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗

    Example 6.4.2.

    You get paid $350 for 6 hours of work. How much would you get paid for 8 hours of work?
    🔗
    Solution: The pay for 8 hours of work is unknown, so represent it by a variable, \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We can organize this problem in a table:
    🔗
    Old New
    Pay $35 \(x\)
    Hours 6 8
    The columns can be turned into ratios, and we can put an = sign between them:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{350}{6} = \frac{x}{8} \end{equation*}
    Now, we can solve this equation by multiplying both sides by 8:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{350}{6}\times 8 = \frac{x}{8}\times 8 \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \frac{350\times 8}{6} = x \end{equation*}
    Since \(x\) is money, it makes sense to write our answer as a decimal:
    \begin{align*} 466.6666666666… \amp =x\\ 466.67 \amp \approx x \end{align*}
    So, if we work 8 hours, we will earn about $466.67.
    🔗
    🔗

    Be Careful!

    When we set up our table and proportion, we always put the quantity we want in the top row. This makes the equation much easier to solve.
    🔗
    🔗
