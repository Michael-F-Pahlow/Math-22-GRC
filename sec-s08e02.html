    Section 8.2 Episode 2: Interval Notation

    Episode 2 Learning Goals.

    • Interpret interval notation.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Convert between inequalities and intervals.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Episode 2 Key Terminology.

    • Interval
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Episode 2 Key Symbols.

    \begin{equation*} \infty \quad \quad \text{as in}\,\, (5,\infty) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Definition 8.2.1.

    Interval notation gives us a different way to represent solutions to inequalities.
    🔗
    🔗
    The interval
    \begin{equation*} [3, 5] \end{equation*}
    refers to all the numbers between 3 and 5, including 3 and including 5. The square brackets communicate that 3 and 5 are included.
    🔗
    The interval [3,5] has the same meaning as the inequality:
    \begin{equation*} 3 \le x \le 5 \end{equation*}
    We can represent this interval on a number line by shading in the region between 3 and 5, drawing a solid dot at 3, and drawing a solid dot at 5. The solid dot communicates that 3 and 5 are included.
    🔗
    By contrast, the interval
    \begin{equation*} (3, 5) \end{equation*}
    refers to all the numbers between 3, not including 3, and not including 5. The parentheses communicate that the 3 and 5 are not included.
    🔗
    The interval (3,5) has the same meaning as the inequality
    \begin{equation*} 3 \lt x \lt 5 \end{equation*}
    We can represent this interval on a number line by shading in the region between 3 and 5, drawing a hollow dot at 3 and a hollow dot at 5.
    🔗

    Be Careful!

    When doing PEMDAS calculations, brackets mean the same thing as parentheses:
    \begin{equation*} 3[4-1] \quad \text{ is the same as}\quad 3(4-1) \end{equation*}
    But when writing intervals, brackets do not mean the same thing as parentheses.
    \begin{equation*} [1,4]\quad \text{ is NOT the same as} \quad (1,4) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.2.4.

    Convert the interval (-2,1] to inequality notation and then draw the interval on a number line.
    🔗
    Solution: This interval means all the numbers between -2 and 1. The number -2 is not included because there is a parenthesis next to it. The number 1 is included, because there is a bracket next to it. So, this would correspond to the inequality
    \begin{equation*} -2 \lt x \le 1 \end{equation*}
    On a number line, we shade the region between -2 and 1, with a hollow dot at -2, and a solid dot at 1.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.2.6.

    Translate the inequality
    \begin{equation*} 1 \le x \lt 7 \end{equation*}
    into interval notation
    🔗
    Solution: The inequality tells us that x is between 1 and 7, including 1, but not including 7. So, this becomes the interval
    \begin{equation*} [1, 7) \end{equation*}
    with a bracket next to 1 and a parenthesis next to 7.
    🔗
    🔗

    Be Careful!

    Notice that the variable itself, \(x\text{,}\) does not appear anywhere in the interval. The interval just shows the smallest and largest values of \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    Intervals are always written with the smaller value on the left side, to make the order of the numbers on the number line.
    🔗

    Example 8.2.7.

    Write the inequality in interval notation:
    \begin{equation*} 4 \ge x \gt 2 \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Solution: This inequality says that \(x\) is between 2 and 4, including 4, not including 2. So, we write the interval
    \begin{equation*} (2,4] \end{equation*}
    (2,4] We put the smaller number, 2, on the left side of the interval. We put a parenthesis next to 2 because it is not included. We put a bracket next to 4 because it is included.
    🔗
    🔗
    The inequality
    \begin{equation*} x \gt 5 \end{equation*}
    means that \(x\) can be anything greater than 5. The interval containing all numbers greater than 5 is written:
    \begin{equation*} (5,\infty) \end{equation*}
    with the infinity symbol, \(\infty\text{,}\) on the right side. We use the infinity symbol to communicate that can be \(x\) as big as you want.
    🔗
    The inequality
    \begin{equation*} x \lt 7 \end{equation*}
    means that \(x\) can be anything less than or equal to 7. The interval containing all numbers less than or equal to 7 is written:
    \begin{equation*} (-7, \infty] \end{equation*}
    with the negative infinity symbol, \(-\infty\text{,}\) on the left side. We use the negative infinity symbol to communicate that \(x\) can be as small as you want.
    🔗

    Example 8.2.8.

    Write the inequality \(x\ge 2\) in interval notation.
    🔗
    Solution: Here, \(x\) can be anything greater than or equal to 2. So, we would write:
    \begin{equation*} [2, \infty) \end{equation*}
    We put a bracket next to the 2 to communicate that 2 is included. We use the infinity symbol to communicate that can be \(x\) as big as you want.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 8.2.9.

    Write the inequality \(x \lt 6\) in interval notation.
    🔗
    Solution: Here, \(x\) can be anything less than 6. So, we would write:
    \begin{equation*} (-\infty, 6) \end{equation*}
    We put a parenthesis next to the 6 to communicate that 6 is not included. We use the negative infinity symbol to communicate that \(x\) can be as small as you want.
    🔗
    🔗

    Be Careful!

    Positive \(\infty\) will always appear on the right side of the interval because it is bigger than everything.
    🔗
    Negative \(\infty\) will always appear on the left side of the interval because it is more negative than everything.
    🔗
    There will ALWAYS be a parenthesis next to positive or negative \(\infty\text{,}\) because \(x\) can never actually equal infinity.
    🔗
    🔗
