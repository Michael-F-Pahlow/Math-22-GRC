    Section 5.4 Episode 4: Calculating Area and Perimeter

    Episode 4 Learning Goals.

    • Use geometry formulas to calculate area and perimeter / circumference for rectangles, triangles, and circles.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Approximate pi as a decimal number.
      🔗
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Episode 4 Key Symbols.

    \begin{equation*} \pi \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Perimeter and Area of a Rectangle
    🔗
    Use the variable L to represent the length of a rectangle and use the variable W to represent the width of a rectangle.
    🔗
    The formula for the perimeter (\(P\)) of the rectangle is:
    \begin{equation*} P = L + W + L + W \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Notice Something Neat!

    Remember that a number to itself is the same as multiplying that number by 2. So, we could also write the perimeter formula this way:
    \begin{equation*} P = 2L + 2W \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The formula for the area (\(A\)) of the rectangle is:
    \begin{equation*} A = LW \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 5.4.2.

    If a rectangle has a length of 4 feet and a width of 3 feet, find its perimeter and area. Include appropriate units in your answer.
    🔗
    Solution: The rectangle’s perimeter is:
    \begin{equation*} P = 2(4) + 2(3) = 8 + 6 = 14 \,\,\text{feet} \end{equation*}
    And its area is
    \begin{equation*} A = 4\times 3 = 12 \,\,\text{square feet} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Perimeter and Area of a Triangle
    🔗
    Use the variables \(s_1\text{,}\) \(s_2\) and \(s_3\) to represent the lengths of the three sides of your triangle. (Note: We are using subscripts on our variables to tell the lengths of the different sides apart.)
    🔗
    Then the perimeter of the triangle can be found by adding together the lengths of all three sides:
    \begin{equation*} P = s_1 + s_1 + s_3 \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 5.4.4.

    If a triangle has sides of lengths 2 centimeters, 3 centimeters, and 4 centimeters, find its perimeter. Include units in your answer.
    🔗
    The triangle’s perimeter is:
    \begin{equation*} P = 2 +3 + 4 = 9\,\,\text{centimeters} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    To find the area of the triangle, we need to start by making sure one side of the triangle is lying horizontal, as if we had set the triangle on a table. The length of the side that’s on the table is called the base, represented by the variable \(b\text{.}\) Then we measure the height of the triangle from the base to the top corner; we represent the height by the variable \(h\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then the area of the triangle can be found using the formula:
    \begin{equation*} A = \frac{1}{2}\times b \times h \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 5.4.6.

    If the triangle has a base of length 4 centimeters and a height of 1.5 centimeters, find its area. Include units in your answer.
    🔗
    Solution: The triangle’s area is:
    \begin{equation*} A = \frac{1}{2}\times 4 \times 1.5 = 3\,\,\text{square centimeters} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    The letter \(\pi\) The letter π (“pi”, read aloud as “pie” as in “apple pie”) represents the area of a circle with a radius of 1. Its value is around 3.14.
    \begin{equation*} \pi \approx 3.14 \end{equation*}
    Representations of \(\pi\) with more decimals like 3.14159 might be needed if you are doing a calculation that requires a lot of precision. The decimals of \(\pi\) can be seen by using the \(\pi\) button that appears on most calculators.
    🔗
    Area and Circumference for a Circle
    🔗
    Suppose a circle has a radius of length \(r\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then the circumference is given by the formula:
    \begin{equation*} C=2\times \pi r \end{equation*}
    And the area is given by the formula:
    \begin{equation*} A = \pi\times r^2 \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Notice Something Neat!

    What if you’re given the diameter of the circle instead of the radius? In that case, you can just cut the diameter in half to find the circle’s radius.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 5.4.7.

    Suppose that a circle has a diameter of 1.2 feet. Find the circle’s circumference and area. Include units in your area.
    🔗
    Solution: We can cut the diameter in half to get the radius:
    \begin{equation*} \frac{1.2\,\,\text{feet}}{2} = 0.6\,\,\text{feet} \end{equation*}
    Now, we can use the formula for the area of a circle, following PEMDAS, and using \pi\approx 3.14:
    \begin{align*} A \amp = \pi\times (0.6)^2\\ \amp = 1.1304 \,\,\text{square feet} \end{align*}
    And we can use the formula for the circumference,
    \begin{align*} C \amp = 2\times \pi \times 0.6 \\ \amp = 3.768\,\,\text{feet} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗
